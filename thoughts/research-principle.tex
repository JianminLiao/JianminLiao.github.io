\textbf{Even when intrinsic value is the sole objective, the optimal strategy begins by devoting all or nearly all resources to external value.}

Two results establish this principle for a sufficiently long research horizon.

Intrinsic value is the scientific contribution the researcher ultimately values. External value is support convertible into research funding, measured in the same units as expenditure.

\section*{Goal}

Maximize expected total intrinsic value over $n$ research cycles:
\[
 \max\ \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_i\right].
\]

\section*{Assumptions for Theorem 1}

Consider $n$ research cycles, starting with a seed budget $c_1=1$. In cycle $i$, expenditure is $c_i$ and the fraction allocated to intrinsic work is $p_i\in[0,1]$. Write $\mathbb E_i$ for expectation conditional on the information available when $p_i$ is chosen, including that choice.

\textbf{1. Linear expected returns.} For every decision history and allocation,
\[
 \mathbb E_i[I_i]=a p_i c_i,\qquad \mathbb E_i[E_i]=w(1-p_i)c_i,\qquad c_{i+1}=d_iE_i.
\]
The constants $a>0$ and $w$ are common to all cycles. The multipliers $d_i\ge1$ are iid and bounded; each draw is independent of the decision information and current external return.

\textbf{2. A growing reserve.} For every decision history and allocation, the lowest possible external return is $b(1-p_i)c_i$, with the same $b$ across cycles. The funding balance obeys
\[
 B_0=0,\qquad B_i=B_{i-1}+E_i-c_i.
\]
For every possible outcome, require
\[
 B_i\ge\rho B_{i-1},\qquad 1<\rho<b.
\]
Research income must cover spending while growing the reserve.

\textbf{3. Decision information.} The researcher observes the current budget and balance and chooses $p_i$ before the current returns and funding multiplier are drawn. Only $b$, $w$, and $\mathbb E[d_i]$ are known about their distributions.

\section*{Full initial external focus}

\begin{theorem}
There is a finite integer $K\ge1$, independent of the horizon $n$, such that whenever $n>K$, the unique optimal decision in each of the first $n-K$ cycles is to allocate all resources to external value:
\[
 p_i=0\qquad(1\le i\le n-K).
\]
\end{theorem}

\section*{Proof of Theorem 1}

Every feasible state satisfies $0\le B\le c$: this holds initially and is preserved because
\[
 0\le B'=B+E-c\le E\le d_iE=c'.
\]
Write $u=(1-p)c$ for external expenditure. The reserve constraint gives the feasible interval
\[
 \frac{c+(\rho-1)B}{b}\le u\le c.
\]

Divide the objective by $a$, giving expected stage reward $c-u$. Independence of the funding multiplier gives
\[
 v=w\mathbb E[d_i]\ge w\ge b>\rho>1,\qquad \mathbb E_i[c_{i+1}]=v(1-p_i)c_i.
\]
At every decision history with state $(c,B)$ and $m$ cycles remaining, the value is $V_m(c,B)=A_m c+D_m B$, with $A_0=D_0=0$. To see this, define $S_m=vA_m+wD_m$. The Bellman objective is
\[
 (1-D_{m-1})c+D_{m-1}B+(S_{m-1}-1)u.
\]
Its maximizing endpoint depends only on $S_{m-1}$, and both endpoints are linear in $(c,B)$, completing the induction.

When $S_{m-1}\le1$, choose the lower endpoint. Starting from $S_0=D_0=0$, this gives
\[
 D_m=D_{m-1}+\frac{(\rho-1)(S_{m-1}-1)}{b}\le0
\]
and
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 S_m={}&\frac{v+w(\rho-1)}{b}S_{m-1}
 +\frac{v(b-1)-w(\rho-1)}{b}\\
 &+(w-v)D_{m-1}
 \ \ge\ \rho S_{m-1}+b-\rho.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Since $\rho>1$ and $b>\rho$, this sequence crosses $1$ in finite time. Let $K=\min\{m\ge1:S_m>1\}$.

Once $S_{m-1}>1$, the unique maximizing choice is $u=c$. Then $D_m=D_{m-1}\le0$ and
\[
 S_m=vS_{m-1}+(w-v)D_{m-1}>S_{m-1}>1.
\]
Thus every decision with more than $K$ cycles remaining uniquely allocates everything to external value. These are precisely the first $n-K$ cycles.\hfill$\square$

\section*{Assumptions for Theorem 2}

Keep the same objective, initial budget and balance, exact budget rule, and growing-reserve requirement. Use the following assumptions for nonlinear returns.

\textbf{1. Random return functions.} Let $F_i(x)$ and $G_i(x)$ be the intrinsic and external returns from expenditure $x$, so
\[
 I_i=F_i(p_i c_i),\qquad E_i=G_i((1-p_i)c_i),\qquad c_{i+1}=d_iE_i.
\]
The nonnegative return functions vanish at zero. The pairs $(F_i,G_i)$ are iid across cycles. The multipliers $d_i\ge1$ are iid, bounded, and independent of the return functions. Allocations are chosen before the current draws. At every feasible state, finite-horizon optimal policies exist and have finite expected value.

\textbf{2. Positive return increments.} Write $f(x)=\mathbb E[F_i(x)]$. For some $m>0$ and every $x,h\ge0$, require
\[
 f(x+h)-f(x)\ge m h,\qquad G_i(x+h)-G_i(x)\ge b h.
\]
The second inequality holds for every realization. At each expenditure $x$, the lowest possible external return is $bx$, with $1<\rho<b$ as before. These conditions allow nonlinear returns with a positive minimum gain from additional investment.

\section*{Approximate initial external focus}

\begin{theorem}
For every horizon $n\ge2$, the first intrinsic share $p_1^{*(n)}$ under any optimal policy satisfies
\[
 0\le p_1^{*(n)}\le\frac{f(1)}{m(b-\rho)b^{\,n-2}}.
\]
Consequently, $p_1^{*(n)}\to0$ as $n\to\infty$: the optimal initial allocation becomes arbitrarily close to full external focus.
\end{theorem}

\section*{Proof of Theorem 2}

As before, $0\le B\le c$. Intrinsic expenditure $t$ is feasible precisely when
\[
 0\le t\le\frac{(b-1)c-(\rho-1)B}{b}.
\]
Let $V_k(c,B)$ denote optimal expected intrinsic value with $k$ cycles remaining. We first show that increasing the state by $(\delta,\eta)$, with $0\le\eta\le\delta$, gives
\[
 V_k(c+\delta,B+\eta)-V_k(c,B)\ge L_k\delta,\qquad L_k=m(b-\rho)b^{\,k-2}.
\]
For $k=1$, optimal intrinsic expenditure is the upper endpoint above. It increases by at least $(1-\rho/b)\delta$, so the lower bound on $f$ gives $L_1\delta$.

For the induction step, use the smaller state's optimal intrinsic expenditure in the larger state. External expenditure increases by $\delta$; the reserve constraint remains satisfied because $b\delta\ge\delta+(\rho-1)\eta$. Couple the two decisions using the same return functions and funding multiplier. The external return increases by some $\Delta E\ge b\delta$. The next-state differences are
\[
 \delta'=d_i\Delta E,\qquad \eta'=\eta+\Delta E-\delta,
\]
so $0\le\eta'\le\delta'$ and $\delta'\ge b\delta$. Current intrinsic returns are identical. The induction hypothesis therefore gives a continuation gain of at least $bL_{k-1}\delta=L_k\delta$.

Finally, at the initial state $(1,0)$, compare an optimal intrinsic expenditure $t=p_1^{*(n)}$ with full external allocation. Full external allocation increases the next budget and balance by $(d_i\Delta E,\Delta E)$, where $\Delta E\ge bt$. The value comparison just proved gives a continuation gain of at least $bL_{n-1}t=L_nt$. Optimality of $t$ requires its current expected intrinsic return to cover this gain:
\[
 L_nt\le f(t)\le f(1).
\]
Dividing by $L_n$ proves the bound. Since $b>1$, its right-hand side tends to zero.\hfill$\square$
